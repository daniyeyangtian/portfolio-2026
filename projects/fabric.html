Overview

A client project in UMSI’s SI 500, with Microsoft Fabric. Our partner leads data science on Fabric, and data science rarely gets to hear from users in their own words. He asked us to bring back qualitative research on “agent-led growth”: what happens to discovery, comparison and evaluation once people hand them to an AI assistant or agent.

Fabric is a data platform-as-a-service for storing, processing and using data, in the same space as Snowflake, Databricks and Palantir. How people use such platforms is changing with agentic and coding tools, and our partner wants agent-led growth to set Fabric apart.

We focused on small and medium businesses, where a platform choice is costly to undo and is rarely one person’s call.

The brief

  1. Go find out what I cannot see from inside Microsoft.
  2. Interview outside my bubble: potential customers, not Microsoft people.
  3. Tell me what is worth measuring, and why.

What changes when people hand software discovery to AI?

How AI is changing the way small businesses discover, compare and choose software.

Being found by an AI is not the same as being found by search.

Discovery used to run through search engines and marketing. Now an assistant often writes the shortlist. Our partner’s question: can a product be built so that an agent arrives at a recommendation it can prove, rather than one it merely asserts?

We asked it of small and medium businesses.

How do SMEs discover, explore and evaluate new digital products, and where does AI change that? We looked at people choosing platforms with high switching costs, who already use AI in their work, not at one-off questions or purchases.

There is no room where the decision gets made

From our expert session on how organisations buy software: a new product moves through a buying group, in no fixed order. The advocate is won by a better fit; almost everyone else, by cost and contract terms.

6–10people in a typical buying group

  • Agents won’t change how companies buyThey buy after a price rise, a lost fit, a better challenger or a change of leadership, as before.
  • Design isn’t a decision driverIt sways the advocate: easier to learn, faster to be productive. It has to show up as outcomes.
  • Switching has a costA better product can still lose to retraining people and migrating data.

Where AI already helps

Almost everyone had used an assistant somewhere in discovery. Very few let it make the call.

What would earn their trust

Inaccurate or outdated answers were the top worry (14 of 25). What helps is evidence they can check.

  • Links to reliable sources16/25
  • Why it was recommended14/25
  • Clear comparisons9/25

Synthesis

We put every interview note and open survey answer on an affinity wall, then grouped and regrouped them into four core findings.

350+ notes · 4 core findings

  1. AI speeds up discovery, alongside human networks, not instead of them.

    • People still find and judge tools through peers, communities and colleagues.
    • AI is the starting point: a quick overview and a shortlist.
    • Its blind spots are hard to see: unclear sources, and tools outside English may be missed.

  2. Adoption depends on fit, authority and cost, not just on a good recommendation.

    • Existing systems and familiar platforms shape what counts as suitable.
    • Access policies and who owns the risk separate using a tool from choosing it.
    • Price sensitivity grows with company size and the scale of the purchase.

  3. Trust needs clear requirements, independent checking and evidence that fits the risk.

    • People keep the decision and cross-check AI before accepting it.
    • Confident answers can still be wrong, outdated or miss key features.
    • The more specific the requirements, the more relevant the answer.

  4. Agents can take on the legwork, but only with real testing and a human in the loop.

    • Good evaluation goes beyond feature lists: does it work in our workflow?
    • Agent workflows save effort, yet people still review and refine the results.
    • Many gather opinions widely but don’t always test the tool themselves.

What’s next

The study is still running. Early directions for Microsoft Fabric, still to be tested:

  1. Opportunity 01Recommendations that show their evidence

    Give agents sources, comparisons and reasons they can cite, so a shortlist can be checked instead of taken on faith.

  2. Opportunity 02Speak to the whole buying group

    The person who discovers a tool is rarely the one who signs. Answer IT, cost and fit questions too.

  3. Opportunity 03Make it easy to try in place

    Trust comes from testing against the existing stack, so free trials and real workflows matter more than features.

Next: more interviews with business owners, then a findings report and a checklist of what Fabric’s product teams should measure.
